Section 2

The business in one minute

  • What it is: an AI intern on your phone. You talk to Ollie (the olive) and give it a job. Six real problems it solves:
    1. Letters nobody understands: snap a council, HMRC or landlord letter; Ollie explains it in your language (Pashto, Urdu, Arabic or English), adds the deadline to your phone and drafts the reply.
    2. Companies struck off for missed filings: Ollie watches Companies House and warns you before a late penalty or a strike-off.
    3. Unpaid invoices: Ollie chases politely and works out the interest the law allows.
    4. Wrong platform fees: Ollie checks Fresha and Deliveroo statements for fees you shouldn't pay.
    5. No website, or a dead one: give Ollie your prices and hours; it builds the site and keeps it updated.
    6. Missed grants: Ollie finds funding that fits a charity, school or business, tracks the dates and starts the form.
    For students: class notes turned into revision (tidy notes, flashcards, a quiz, an open-book index and a list of anything in the notes that looks wrong), a draft checked against the brief, deadlines, a deposit the landlord didn't protect, missing bursary payments and payslips that pay too little.
  • What makes it different: it hands you the finished thing (the reply, the website, the date in your calendar), not just a chat answer; a "jury" (law, money, wording) checks every result and shows its source; nothing is sent without your approval; Ollie learns from your corrections.
  • Who pays: businesses £20 a month; students free with a small paid extra.
  • Where it starts: Glasgow and Lanarkshire, then the rest of the UK.
  • Status: working demo built (November 2026), pilot planned with Glasgow salons and students.

Sole trader No. The business is me. Me, without limit, including my home and savings. Register with HMRC for Self Assessment. No Companies House filing. Simple: one tax return a year. Easy start, but too risky for an app that handles people's data.
Partnership (Scottish firm) Yes, in Scotland. Partnership Act 1890, s4(2) (in Scotland a firm is a legal person distinct from the partners of whom it is composed). Limited liability partnership (LLP) Yes. Limited Liability Partnerships Act 2000, s1(2) (an LLP is a body corporate with legal personality separate from that of its members). The LLP itself; members' liability is limited. Incorporate at Companies House with two or more members (Limited Liability Partnerships Act 2000, s2(1)(a) (two or more persons associated for carrying on a lawful business with a view to profit must subscribe the incorporation document)). Accounts and a confirmation statement every year. Ruled out: I'm one person.

An "asset lock" and a yearly community interest report. Built for social purposes; Unpaid Intern is a profit-making tech business.
Scottish charitable incorporated organisation (SCIO) Yes. The SCIO. Apply to OSCR (the Scottish Charity Regulator). Charity rules; profits can't go to owners. Not a charity.

Section 4 · My choice

Unpaid Intern Ltd

Why a private limited company

  1. 1
    It protects me personally. The company is a separate legal person: Salomon v A Salomon & Co Ltd [1897] AC 22 (a company is a separate legal person from its members). If the business owes money or is sued, it is the company's problem, not my home or savings. That matters for an app that handles people's files and gives AI checks.
  2. 2
    It's ready to grow. I can bring in a co-founder or investors later by issuing shares, without changing the business.
  3. 3
    Trust. Businesses handing over their statements and invoices trust a registered "Ltd" with a public record more than an unregistered name.
  4. 4
    It works with one person. I can be the only shareholder and the only director.

The trade-offs I accept

  • More paperwork: yearly accounts and a confirmation statement, filed on time.
  • Directors' duties (section 6 below).
  • My name and a service address go on the public register.
  • Limited liability isn't total: banks often ask a new director for a personal guarantee, and a director can still be personally liable for wrongdoing (for example wrongful trading when insolvent).

Section 5

Setting it up, step by step

  1. Step 1 · Name

    Check the name. Companies House won't register a name that's the same as an existing one (Companies Act 2006, s66 (a company must not be registered by a name that is the same as another name on the index)). I also search the UK trade mark register: "Unpaid Intern" is distinctive, not descriptive, so it can be protected (Trade Marks Act 1994, s3(1)(c) (marks made only of signs that describe the kind or purpose of the goods or services shall not be registered)).

  2. Step 2 · Identity

    Verify my identity. Since 18 November 2025 every new director must have their identity verified with Companies House before acting (Companies Act 2006, s167M, inserted by the Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act 2023 (an individual must not act as a director unless their identity is verified)).

  3. Step 3 · £100

    Incorporate online at Companies House: £100 (from 1 February 2026). Registered office in Scotland, so the company is registered in Scotland. Use the model articles. Name me as director and shareholder, and as the person with significant control.

  4. Step 4 · Corporation Tax

    Register for Corporation Tax with HMRC within 3 months of starting to do business. Rates: 19% on profits up to £50,000; 25% above £250,000, with marginal relief in between.

  5. Step 5 · Bank

    Open a business bank account (the company's money is not my money).

  6. Step 6 · £52 a year

    Pay the ICO data protection fee: £52 a year for a micro organisation (£47 by direct debit), because the business processes personal data.

  7. Step 7 · Privacy

    Write the privacy notice and terms: what data we use, why, how long, and the user's rights (UK GDPR, Article 13 (people must be told how their data is used when it is collected)). The terms say the app gives information to help you decide, with sources, not legal or financial advice.

  8. Step 8 · Website

    Put the company details on the website: registered name, number, part of the UK where it's registered and registered office (Company, Limited Liability Partnership and Business (Names and Trading Disclosures) Regulations 2015, regs 24 and 25 (a company must show its registered name and particulars, including its number, on its websites)).

  9. Step 9 · £90,000

    Later, when needed: VAT once taxable turnover passes £90,000 in 12 months; PAYE and employers' liability insurance before hiring the first employee.

Section 6

Running it: the legal challenges

a. My duties as a director

(Companies Act 2006, ss171-177):

  • s171 (act within the company's constitution and use powers for their proper purpose)
  • s172 (promote the success of the company for the benefit of its members, considering employees, customers, the community and the environment)
  • s173 (exercise independent judgment)
  • s174 (exercise reasonable care, skill and diligence)
  • s175 (avoid conflicts of interest)
  • s176 (not accept benefits from third parties)
  • s177 (declare an interest in a proposed transaction)

Plus filing accounts and a confirmation statement every year (£50 online).

b. Data protection

Unpaid Intern handles people's letters, statements, invoices and essays, and some of that is read by online AI services. The design answers the law: each job collects only what it needs (UK GDPR, Article 5(1)(c) (data must be adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary)), privacy is built in from the start (UK GDPR, Article 25 (data protection by design and by default)), and every AI service works for me under a written contract (UK GDPR, Article 28(3)(a) (a processor may process personal data only on documented instructions from the controller)).

c. When the AI is wrong

If someone relies on a careless statement, the maker can be liable in delict: Hedley Byrne & Co Ltd v Heller & Partners Ltd [1964] AC 465 (a duty of care can arise for a careless statement where the maker assumes responsibility and the other party reasonably relies on it). So: maths and rules are done in code, every result shows its source, the user approves everything, and nothing is sent automatically. A warning from the courts: in R (Ayinde) v London Borough of Haringey [2025] EWHC 1383 (Admin) (lawyers who put AI-invented cases before the court faced wasted costs and referral to their regulators), an English decision, persuasive in Scotland. That is why every Ollie answer links to its real source.

d. Selling to the public (students)

Online subscriptions are distance contracts, so consumers get a 14-day right to cancel (Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013, reg 29 (a consumer may cancel a distance contract within the cancellation period without giving a reason)). Marketing must not mislead (Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024, s226 (giving false or misleading information, including true information presented misleadingly, is a misleading action)). New subscription-contract rules under the 2024 Act are due to start later; to be checked before launch.

e. Brand and intellectual property

Register "Unpaid Intern" and Ollie as trade marks. The Ollie artwork was made with an AI image tool; for computer-generated work, the author is the person who made the arrangements for it (Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988, s9(3) (the author of a computer-generated work is the person by whom the arrangements necessary for its creation are undertaken)), which is me, subject to the tool's terms.

f. The name joke (and a real point)

A human unpaid intern who counts as a worker must be paid (National Minimum Wage Act 1998, s1(1) (a person who qualifies for the national minimum wage must be paid at least that rate)). Ollie is software, not a worker. It doesn't need money; it needs your time.

g. Advice the law keeps for qualified people

Explaining a letter is information, but some letters are about immigration, and immigration advice is restricted: Immigration and Asylum Act 1999, s84(1) (no person may provide immigration advice or immigration services unless he is a qualified person). Breaking that is a crime: Immigration and Asylum Act 1999, s91(1) (providing immigration advice or services in breach of s84 is an offence, with up to two years in prison). Limited liability does not shield the director here: Immigration and Asylum Act 1999, s91(3) (if a company commits the offence with an officer's consent or connivance, or through his neglect, the officer is guilty too). So Ollie only explains what an immigration letter says and points to a qualified adviser.

h. Companies House deadlines

The strike-off job rests on real rules: late accounts bring an automatic penalty for a private company, from £150 (not more than 1 month late) up to £1,500 (more than 6 months) (Companies House, Late filing penalties, GOV.UK, updated 16 January 2026), and Companies House can strike off a company that looks inactive (Companies Act 2006, s1000 (if the registrar has reasonable cause to believe a company is not carrying on business, the registrar may write to ask, and can move to strike it off)). The same rules apply to Unpaid Intern Ltd.
